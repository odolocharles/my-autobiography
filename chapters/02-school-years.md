# School Years

We lived most of our lives along the River Yala, and I spent most of my schooling years around Bondo.

My father never held a steady job that could reliably support and educate us, and there was no one else in a position to step in and help — so the weight of the family fell on my mother. There wasn't much economic activity in our area beyond weeding and ploughing fields for wealthier neighbors, and whatever small wages came from that had to be spent wisely just to get by.

Family stress and poverty, among other issues, pushed my father into excessive alcohol use. He became lazy, unproductive, and irresponsible — and my mother ended up carrying the full weight of the household, including feeding and caring for him as well as us. And yet, even in that state, he would always tell us to work smart. He genuinely believed that we — being bright kids — could someday make it out of our circumstances. That belief earned him a nickname around the neighborhood and his workplaces: *Ti Mariek*, Luo for "work smart."

Meanwhile, my humble mother went out and worked in the farms herself, despite rejection and abuse from village women who felt a teacher had no business doing "dirty" manual labor. That humbled all of us deeply — especially me, being the eldest and the only son.

I finished my primary education in March 2021. It wasn't an easy road — COVID-19 and severe floods disrupted the academic calendar nationwide, pushing our final KCPE (Kenya Certificate of Primary Education) exams to March of the following year, and our high school intake to September that same year. Despite everything, I scored a B+ (372/500).

The holiday after my KCPE was one of the hardest periods of my life. My parents separated — a fight that had actually begun before my exams even started, and it affected my performance. After some legal proceedings, my sisters and I chose to go with our mother. Living with my father wasn't an option; he was often depressed, angry, drunk, and at times violent — we couldn't see a future for ourselves there.

We were fortunate in one respect: our mother was officially employed by the TSC (Teachers Service Commission), which meant she could take out loans to finance our education now that she had a government salary behind her.
